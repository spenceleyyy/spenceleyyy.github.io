EEG Theta Power Varies across Roles in Mixed Human-Robot Teams

Robert J. Spenceley, Aakash Yadav , & Ranjana Mehta

Search and rescue (SAR) tasks are some of the most dangerous and cognitively demanding tasks of emergency response. Cognitive demands vary across team members as role-specific duties introduce variable levels of cognitive load. The inclusion of robotic assistance has shown utility in providing recommendations, updating information, and alerting operators in SAR operations. Without perfect reliability, the responder may be a victim of automation bias, additional cognitive load, or lack of trust. Neuroimaging using Electroencephalography (EEG) can provide insights into how changing a teammate’s attributes alters others’ brain functions. This study aims to characterize the impact of different team roles on EEG frequency bands.

Forty participants from a large, public university completed a virtual SAR task in 20 teams, completing Human-Human (HHH), Human-Robot Reliable (HHR), and Human-Robot Unreliable (HRU) conditions. EEG data was collected using a 32-electrode cap and analyzed for theta power at frontal (FP1, FP2) and temporoparietal (TP9, TP10) sites. Separate Kruskal-Wallis tests assessed differences between team conditions for each role.

Among commanders, higher theta power was observed in HRU conditions at FP2 and TP10, while safety officers showed lower theta power at FP1 in the same condition. Increases in frontal theta in commanders indicate adaptations to task demands, while decreases in safety officers suggest that joint agency was not met. These results suggest that neural adaptation may be role-dependent, with commanders adapting better to unreliable automation, highlighting implications for optimizing robotic assistance in high-stakes response teams.

Citation: Spenceley, R. J., Yadav, A., & Mehta, R. K. (2024). EEG theta power varies across roles in mixed human-robot teams. Poster presented at the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting.

FIPRED: A Study of Predictive Automated Displays for Wildfire Fighting Management

Robert J. Spenceley, Christopher D. Wickens , & Bianca de Toledo Piza

Predictor displays are a form of automation that have been shown to provide a notable benefit in many environments and are applied to wildfire suppression in the present study. 22 participants were recruited to participate in a wildfire prediction study in which they predicted the fire trajectory based on the automated predictor and their interpretation of the raw data. In all scenarios, a large predictor benefit was observed, and in four of six scenarios, significant signs of automation bias were found, following an erroneous automated prediction.

Participants performed significantly worse when the raw data influences on the trajectory were removed in a control study, signaling the value of such data in calibrating dependence on the predictor. The FIPRED display was beneficial to participants, improving their ability to predict the future position of the fire. However, the automation bias indicated that participants may have over-relied on the automated assistance.

Components of Executive Functioning Involved in Prospective Memory Performance

Robert J. Spenceley, Michaela Rice , & Deana Davalos

Prospective memory (PM) involves setting intentions and remembering to complete tasks, a process essential for college students to manage daily responsibilities. Executive functioning supports these abilities through planning, monitoring, and managing attention, and prior research suggests the two are closely linked. Using the Dysexecutive Questionnaire (DEX), this study examined which components of executive functioning predict PM performance across five factors: inhibition, interference management, intentionality, planning, and social regulation.

Results from seventy-nine participants showed that greater inhibition impairment was associated with lower PM accuracy, while other factors were unrelated. These findings suggest that inhibition plays a key role in PM, supporting the PAM model and emphasizing the importance of self-monitoring and attentional control in remembering intended actions.

Citation: Spenceley, R. J., Rice, M., & Davalos, D. (2023). Components of executive functioning involved in prospective memory performance. Poster presented at the Rocky Mountain Psychological Association Annual Conference.
